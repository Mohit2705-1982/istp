Front Matter

Abstract & Project Overview

An academic field study on preserving the living cultural memory of the Himalayan region

Abstract

The Himalayan region of Himachal Pradesh harbors one of the richest concentrations of living cultural heritage in South Asia — encompassing centuries-old temple traditions, Tibetan Buddhist monastic practices, rare manuscript collections, traditional Sowa-Rigpa medicine, and intangible oral knowledge systems transmitted across generations through direct human contact.

This Interactive Socio-Technical Practicum (ISTP) report presents findings from three structured field visits: Site Visit 1 — Mandi ("Choti Kashi"), Site Visit 2 — Dharamshala/McLeodganj, and Site Visit 3 — Rewalsar (Tso Pema), conducted during the academic year 2024–25. Through direct observation, semi-structured interviews with local priests, monks, traditional doctors (Amchis), and community members, this study documents key practices, identifies threats to their continuity, and proposes actionable recommendations for digital and community-based archiving.

Our findings reveal a significant generational gap in cultural transmission, the fragility of oral knowledge systems, and the urgent need for collaborative archiving efforts involving local communities, academic institutions, and government bodies. The study highlights the profound syncretic nature of Himachali and Tibetan traditions — most vividly demonstrated at Rewalsar, where Buddhist, Hindu, and Sikh pilgrims share one of the most sacred landscapes in the Himalayan world.

Research Design

Methodology

A multi-modal qualitative and observational research approach across three field sites

Literature Review

Reviewed existing scholarship on Himachali temple traditions, Tibetan manuscript culture, Sowa-Rigpa, and oral heritage preservation. Consulted ASI reports, cultural studies, and government heritage documents.

Questionnaire Design

Developed an 8-section structured fieldwork questionnaire covering folk culture, ritual practices, manuscript preservation, archiving methods, and modern challenges — deployed across all three field sites.

① Site Visit 1 — Mandi

Documented 8+ temples including Panchvaktra, Trilokinath, Bhootnath, Tarna Devi, Neelkanth Mahadev, Bhimakali, khua Rani, and Shambhu Ghat. Interviewed temple priests, local historians, and ASI officials.

② Site Visit 2 — Dharamshala

Visited monasteries in McLeodganj and Men-Tsee-Khang. Interviewed monks (Tenzin, Tsering, Choekyi), Amchi doctors, and cultural guides. Documented Sowa-Rigpa, life-cycle rituals, and monastic education.

③ Site Visit 3 — Rewalsar

Visited Tso Pema (Lotus Lake). Documented the syncretic heritage of Buddhist monasteries, Hindu shrines, and Sikh Gurudwara. Recorded oral accounts of the Padmasambhava–Mandarava legend and observed living pilgrimage practices across faith traditions.

Data Analysis & Reporting

Transcribed interviews, coded field notes thematically across all three sites, compiled documentation, and synthesized findings into structured recommendations for digital archiving and policy advocacy.

Site Visit 1 — Mandi

Temples of Mandi — Choti Kashi

Panchvaktra Mahadev Temple
Panchvaktra Mahadev Temple, Mandi — 16th Century

Panchvaktra Mahadev Temple

Sanctum of the Five-Faced Shiva — ASI Protected Monument

Situated on the sacred banks of the Vipasha River, the Panchvaktra Mahadev Temple is one of the most remarkable Shaiva shrines in Himachal Pradesh. Constructed in the 16th century — contemporaneous with the Trilokinath Temple — it was restored and declared a protected monument by ASI in the 1980s.

The north-facing temple enshrines Lord Shiva in his five-faced (Panchmukhi) form. The five faces — Aghora, Ishaan, Tatpurush, Vamdev, and Rudra — represent the spread of divine consciousness in all directions. According to Tantric scriptures, this form is identified with the fourth Mahavidya, Devi Shodashi (Rajarajeshwari).

The sanctum houses a five-faced Shivalinga, while the mandapa displays the Panchmukhi Shiva idol with hair (jata) in the yogic form. A remarkable sculpture depicts Goddess Parvati embracing Shiva — representing the Ardhanarishvara aspect and the creative union of Shakti with Shiva. The temple architecture reflects the harmonious coexistence of Vaishnav and Shaiv traditions.

Trilokinath Temple — Built 1520 CE, Mandi

Trilokinath Temple

The Three-Faced Shiva — Built 1520 CE — ASI Protected

Standing on the sacred banks of the Vipasha River, the Trilokinath Temple predates the founding of the town of Mandi itself. An inscription in Tankari script on the sanctum's outer wall dates its construction to Kali Samvat 2264, Shaka Samvat 1442 (1520 CE), commissioned by Queen Sultana Devi, consort of Raja Ajbar Sen of the Sen Rajput dynasty.

The sanctum houses a life-sized three-faced idol of Shiva, seated on Nandi Maharaj with Parvati carved on his left. A rare Panchamukhi idol in the mandapa features five faces — three visible, one facing the back wall, and one hidden at the top — representing the esoteric spiritual traditions of the Bengal-origin Sen kings.

The temple complex also enshrines Goddesses Narada and Sharda — considered awakened deities (Jagrit Devis) and the family deities (Kuldevis) of Mandi's people. According to temple priest Ramesh, the three-faced idol was consecrated through Jeev Pratishtha and is believed to have moved at night during the princely era.

Bhootnath Temple
Bhootnath Temple — 1527 CE — Nagara Style

Bhootnath Temple

Lord Shiva's Eternal Abode — Founded 1527 CE

The Bhootnath Temple, located in Chohatta Bazar (Bhootnath Gali), is Mandi's most iconic Shiva temple. Founded in 1527 CE by Raja Ajber Sen — the founder of Mandi town — it stands as a masterpiece of Nagara-style architecture with its soaring Shikhara visible across the town.

According to legend, Raja Ajber Sen dreamed of a cow pouring milk onto a spot in the forest. Excavation revealed a naturally-formed Shivalinga — interpreted as a divine sign — upon which the temple was constructed. The Shivalinga, enshrined in the central sanctum (garbhagriha), has been continuously worshipped for nearly 500 years.

The temple is the epicenter of Mandi's celebrated International Shivratri Festival — a week-long event drawing deities from 200+ temples across the district. The Shivalinga is bathed in Panchamrit, and the courtyard transforms into a vibrant cultural arena blending devotion and tradition.

Tarna Devi Temple
Tarna Devi Temple — 17th Century — Hilltop Shrine

Tarna Devi Temple

Shyama Kali — Victory Monument of King Shyam Sen

Perched dramatically on a hilltop overlooking Mandi, the Tarna Devi Temple is dedicated to Devi Tarana Shyama Kali — regarded by the people of Mandi as "essential as life itself." Built in the 17th century by King Shyam Sen (r. 1664–1676), it serves as both a spiritual sanctuary and a victory monument commemorating the Lohara Battle against Suket.

The name "Tarana" derives from the Sanskrit meaning "one who helps cross the ocean of existence." The north-facing sanctum houses a natural stone idol of the Goddess, whose tongue protrudes and whose form radiates fierce compassion. The Tamasic energy associated with this west-facing Kali temple historically included animal sacrifice practices.

King Balveer Sen later embellished the temple with exquisite Meenakari work — craftsmen from Jaipur applied gold, silver, and vivid colors to stone ceilings and walls, depicting Ganesha, Shiva, and Kali in the Kundan art tradition.

Neelkanth Mahadev Temple

The Blue-Throated Shiva — Shikhara Style with Royal Stepwell

The Neelkanth Mahadev Temple enshrines Lord Shiva in his Neelkanth (blue-throated) form — commemorating Shiva's act of consuming the cosmic poison during the churning of the ocean (Samudra Manthan). Built in the Shikhara architectural style on a raised platform facing east, the temple features a Shiva Linga with a prominent serpent sculpture coiled around it.

The sanctum entrance displays a remarkable sculpture of Lord Indra riding an elephant. Adjacent stands the royal-era stepwell "Pehru Ri Baay" — carved in Shikhara style with a circumambulatory path — and below it the stepped water tank "Jaichu Naun", a historic sacred water source for the townspeople of Mandi.

Bhimakali Temple
Bhimakali Temple — Est. 1660 CE — Vipasha Riverbank

Bhimakali Temple

Kuldevi of Road Brahmins — Built 1660–1666 CE

Situated on a high mound along the banks of the Vipasha River, the Bhimakali Temple enshrines the natural form of the Goddess Bhimakali as a pindi (sacred stone idol) — considered the Kuldevi (family deity) of the Road Brahmin families who migrated to Mandi alongside the Sen Rajput rulers.

The temple carries a dramatic legend: opponents of idol worship threw the sacred pindi into the Vipasha River. The Goddess appeared in a vision to her devotee Sahdev, directing him to retrieve the pindi. Sahdev recovered the idol from the river and reinstalled it on the mound — a miraculous restoration that deepened community devotion.

Construction began on September 26, 1660 and was completed on May 28, 1666, with significant contributions from devotee Tara Kapoor. Until the 1980s, the surrounding area contained remnants of ancient sculptures and Shivalingas.

Mata Kuan Rani Temple
Mata Kuan Rani Temple — Sacred to Both Faiths

Mata Kuan Rani Temple

Princess of the Well — Sacred to Buddhists & Hindus — Gateway to Rewalsar

The Mata Kuan Rani Temple ("Princess of the Well Temple") stands near the bank of the Beas River in Mandi, built over a deep well on a slate-roofed shrine. It marks the legendary site where Princess Mandarava — daughter of King Arshadhara of the ancient kingdom of Zahor (identified with Mandi) — was imprisoned by her father.

Born an "Awareness Dakini," Mandarava refused all marriage offers, was ordained by the Buddhist scholar Śāntarakṣita, and became the spiritual consort and disciple of Padmasambhava (Guru Rinpoche). When the king condemned them both to death in a fire that raged for seven days, the flames transformed into the sacred lake of Rewalsar (Tso Pema) — directly connecting this temple to our third field site.

This temple is uniquely sacred to both Tibetan Buddhists and Hindus, and was historically a pilgrimage stop for Tibetan traders and pilgrims before the closure of the Tibet-India border.

Shambhu Ghat
Shambhu Ghat — Sacred Riverside Site, Mandi

Shambhu Ghat

Sacred Riverside Shivalinga — Near Paddal Ground

Shambhu Ghat, located near the New Bridge on the banks of the Beas River adjacent to the Paddal Ground, is a serene and spiritually charged site featuring a large Shivalinga dedicated to Lord Shiva. The riverside setting amplifies the meditative quality of this space, making it a frequented site for both ritual practice and quiet contemplation.

Devotees come to Shambhu Ghat for ritual baths (snan) in the sacred Beas before or after temple visits, offering prayers at the Shivalinga. The natural and unadorned character of the ghat distinguishes it from the ornate temples nearby, representing the more personal and direct dimension of Shaiva worship in Mandi.

The flowing Beas River at this point carries deep symbolic importance — identified with the ancient Vedic river Vipasha, it is the axis around which Mandi's entire sacred geography is organized. Numerous important temples, including Trilokinath and Panchvaktra, are situated along its banks.

Site Visit 2 — Dharamshala

Tibetan Cultural Practices

Living traditions documented through community interviews in McLeodganj

Primary interviews with community members: Tsering, Choekyi, and Tenzin — residents and monks of the McLeodganj Tibetan settlement
🕌

Monastic Daily Life

  • Morning Prayer: 6:30 AM – 8:30 AM
  • Secondary Session: 8:40 AM – 9:00 AM
  • Discourse/Sermon: 9:00 AM – 11:00 AM
  • Monlam Puja: Annual 5-day prayer festival for the deceased — akin to Hindu Shradh
  • Education includes English, Hindi, Maths, and Buddhist History/Philosophy
  • Anyone (at any age, if unmarried) may choose to become a monk
💍

Life Cycle Rituals

  • Naming: Formal name given by Guru/Lama for spiritual alignment
  • Marriage: 3-day ceremony presided over by a Lama
  • Pangden: Traditional striped apron — worn only by married women
  • No Haldi ceremony; ring exchange is central
  • Traditional attire and jewelry vary by village/region
  • Core rituals remain mandatory despite modern Western influences
🕯️

Death & Afterlife Beliefs

  • 3-Day Rule: Soul believed to stay in the body for 3 days after death
  • Funeral timing calculated from birth date — may be delayed up to a week
  • 49-Day Ritual: Prayers every 7th day; food set aside daily for the deceased
  • Sattu offering: Wheat, sugar, honey, and curd burnt in fire — soul "consumes" the essence through smoke
  • Prayers performed to guide and release the transitioning soul
⭐

Astrology & Cultural Calendar

  • Losar: Tibetan New Year (typically February) — date varies as Tibetan calendar adds extra month every 3 years
  • Kundli: Life map for newborns — predicts lifespan, identifies planetary obstacles
  • Saga Dawa: 4th month (May) — dedicated to Avalokiteshvara prayers
  • Gaden Ngamchoe: Festival of lights akin to Diwali — marks a great Guru's passing
  • No caste system or "untouchability" — all are fundamentally equal
🧿

Protection Rituals & Mantras

  • Kala Tika: Black mark behind ear/forehead to ward off evil eye — used until age 4–5
  • Tunga Amulet: Consecrated by Gurus — contains sacred stone/deity for protection
  • Kala Chakra: Thread/amulet distributed after major pujas
  • Green Tara Mantra: Om Tare Tuttare Ture Soha — for overcoming obstacles and bad dreams
  • Medicine Buddha Mantra: Recited while taking herbal medicines to enhance their efficacy
🌿

Nature, Elements & Modernity

  • Four Elements: Sa (Earth), Chu (Water), Me (Fire), Lung (Wind) — mantras chanted to mitigate natural disasters
  • Older generations follow Guru's word with deep faith; younger generation is more analytical
  • Dalai Lama encourages testing teachings through personal experience
  • Traditions tied to the narrative of the struggle for Tibetan freedom
  • Cultural identity remains strong despite diaspora context

Pulse Diagnosis & Urine Analysis

Amchis (traditional Tibetan doctors) diagnose patients through pulse examination on both wrists, identifying internal organ conditions and energy imbalances. Urine analysis—based on color, odor, and bubble formation—is also used as a key diagnostic tool rooted in ancient Sowa-Rigpa texts.

Himalayan Medicinal Flora

Several high-altitude plants are widely used: Bichu Butti (Nettle) for diabetes and blood pressure, Buransh (Rhododendron) for fever and liver health, Devdar decoctions for joint pain and recovery, and Barberry (Daru-Haldi) for wound healing. Triphala (Haritaki, Bibhitaki, Amalaki) supports digestion.

Rare Herbs & Regional Phenomena

Unique Himalayan ecology supports rare species like Gucchi (Morels), a highly valued mushroom growing under pine trees. Certain plants bloom once in 14 years, tied to local legends. Bana (Nirgundi) is used for sprains and joint pain through steam or paste application.

Sacred Medicines & Remedies

Medicines are derived from herbs, minerals, and sometimes purified metals. Mani Rilbu are sacred pills blessed by Gurus, while Shindeem is used for respiratory issues. Simple remedies like ginger water with honey are used for common illnesses.

Spiritual & Ethical Healing

Healing integrates spirituality through invocation of the Medicine Buddha (Bhaisajyaguru). Doctors emphasize compassion and lifestyle correction, believing many illnesses arise from improper diet and habits rather than just physical causes.

Men-Tsee-Khang Institute

The Men-Tsee-Khang Institute in Dharamshala is the central hub of Sowa-Rigpa medicine. It combines traditional knowledge with modern relevance, treating chronic conditions like arthritis, digestive disorders, and stress-related illnesses.

③ Site Visit 3 — Rewalsar

Rewalsar — Tso Pema

The Lotus Lake — our third field site, where Hindu, Buddhist & Sikh traditions converge in sacred legend

③
Third Field Visit
Rewalsar (Tso Pema) was visited as our third and final field site, approximately 24 km from Mandi. This visit focused on documenting the inter-faith syncretic heritage of the sacred Lotus Lake — its Buddhist monasteries, Hindu shrines, Sikh Gurudwara, oral legends of Padmasambhava and Princess Mandarava, and the living pilgrimage traditions of multiple communities co-existing at one sacred site.

The Sacred Lotus Lake

Situated approximately 24 km from Mandi, Rewalsar Lake — known in Tibetan as Tso Pema ("Lotus Lake") — is one of the most significant inter-faith pilgrimage sites in Himachal Pradesh, simultaneously sacred to Tibetan Buddhists, Hindus, and Sikhs. This third visit allowed our team to observe how multiple living faith traditions co-exist at a single sacred landscape.

The lake is inseparably linked to the legend of Princess Mandarava of Zahor (Mandi) — documented at the Kuan Rani Temple during our first field visit — and Padmasambhava (Guru Rinpoche). When the king condemned them to death in a pyre that burned for seven days, the flames transformed into this lake, with a lotus blooming at its center. This miracle convinced the king, who sought teachings from Padmasambhava and granted his daughter freedom to pursue her spiritual destiny.

Buddhist Significance: Multiple monasteries, prayer flags, and a large statue of Padmasambhava overlook the lake. Tibetan pilgrims circumambulate the lake reciting mantras — Vajrayana practitioners consider it a sacred power site (piṭha). Monks from McLeodganj (Site 2) regularly undertake pilgrimage here.

Hindu Significance: The lake is associated with the sage Lomas and has temples dedicated to local Hindu deities. Hindu pilgrims perform rituals here, particularly during Maha Shivratri — directly connecting to the temple tradition documented in our Mandi visit (Site 1).

Floating Islands & Sacred Carp: The lake is famous for small floating reed islands that shift position — considered sacred by all communities. Sacred carp fish are considered divine and fed by pilgrims of all faiths, representing a unique shared ritual practice that transcends religious boundaries.

Historical Pilgrimage Route: Before the closure of the Tibet-India border, Rewalsar was a significant stop for Tibetan pilgrims and traders — a historical corridor connecting the Dharamshala Tibetan community (Site 2) with the sacred geography of Mandi (Site 1).

Data Analysis — All Three Sites

Key Findings & Observations

Thematic synthesis from field interviews, direct observation, and community interaction across Mandi, Dharamshala & Rewalsar

01

Oral Knowledge in Critical Decline

The most urgent finding across all three sites is the rapid erosion of oral knowledge systems. Traditional priests, Amchis, and elders hold complex ritual knowledge, medical formulations, and mythological narratives that are not systematically recorded. As senior knowledge-holders pass away without successors, entire bodies of living knowledge disappear irreversibly. Younger generations, increasingly urban and digitally connected, show limited interest in traditional apprenticeship.

02

Profound Syncretic Traditions Across All Sites

All three field sites demonstrate remarkable inter-religious synergy. The Kuan Rani Temple in Mandi (Site 1) and Rewalsar Lake (Site 3) are both sacred to Tibetan Buddhists and Hindus, directly linked through the Mandarava–Padmasambhava legend. The Trilokinath Temple synthesizes Shaivism and Vaishnavism. Rewalsar additionally includes a Sikh Gurudwara — making it a unique three-faith convergence point requiring cross-community archiving approaches.

03

Sowa-Rigpa Under Pressure

Traditional Tibetan medicine (Sowa-Rigpa) practiced at Men-Tsee-Khang (Site 2) remains the primary healthcare preference for chronic conditions. However, knowledge of high-altitude herb identification, collection seasonality, and purification of mineral ingredients is concentrated among a small number of trained Amchis. The medicinal herb supply chain from the Himalayan highlands is increasingly disrupted by climate change and restricted collection access.

04

Architectural Heritage at Risk — Site 1

While ASI protection has helped conserve major temples like Panchvaktra and Trilokinath, a significant number of smaller shrines and rural village temples remain unprotected and unmapped. Earthquake damage (two of five subsidiary shrines at Trilokinath were destroyed) and neglect continue to erode architectural heritage. The intricate Meenakari work at Tarna Devi Temple and the Tankari script inscriptions at Trilokinath require specialized conservation urgently.

05

Rewalsar — The Living Syncretic Pilgrimage

Our third field visit to Rewalsar (Tso Pema) revealed a uniquely resilient form of cultural heritage — the shared pilgrimage tradition. Despite modernization pressures, Buddhists, Hindus, and Sikhs continue to co-exist and perform their respective rituals at the lake. The sacred carp-feeding ritual and circumambulation of the lake by multiple faith traditions represent a form of intangible heritage that is self-sustaining but needs formal documentation before demographic shifts alter its character.

06

Generational Gap in Cultural Transmission

A clear generational divide exists across all three sites. In the Tibetan community, elders follow Guru's guidance with deep faith while modern-educated youth approach traditions analytically and selectively. In Mandi, young people participate in Shivratri but have limited knowledge of iconographic traditions. The Dalai Lama's encouragement of analytical engagement is helping bridge this gap — but structural transmission mechanisms are increasingly difficult to sustain in urban contexts.

Future Work — All Three Sites

Recommendations

Actionable pathways for preserving the living cultural heritage of Mandi, Dharamshala & Rewalsar

Interoperable Digital Archive

Establish a centralized, open-access digital repository for Himachali and Tibetan cultural heritage following international standards. IIT Mandi, in collaboration with Men-Tsee-Khang, ASI, and the Rewalsar monastery council, is ideally positioned to lead this initiative spanning all three sites.

Oral Knowledge Recording Program

Launch a systematic oral history documentation program targeting temple priests (Site 1), Amchis and monks (Site 2), and pilgrimage guides and faith community leaders (Site 3). Prioritize recording the Mandarava legend traditions linking Mandi and Rewalsar.

Academic–Community Partnerships

Formalize MoUs between IIT Mandi, Men-Tsee-Khang, the HP State Museum, monastery libraries, and the inter-faith trust at Rewalsar. Annual ISTP student documentation cycles across all three sites would ensure continuity of archiving efforts.

Manuscript Conservation Labs

Establish dedicated conservation labs for the physical stabilization and digitization of manuscripts — particularly the Tankari-script inscriptions at Trilokinath (Site 1), Tibetan Buddhist texts at McLeodganj (Site 2), and oral legend documentation at Rewalsar (Site 3).

Policy Advocacy for ASI Coverage

Advocate for extending ASI protection to currently unprotected sites including Bhimakali Temple, Tarna Devi Temple, the Neelkanth stepwell complex (all Site 1), and the lakeside heritage structures at Rewalsar (Site 3). Develop a comprehensive heritage mapping survey of Mandi's 81+ temples.

Inter-faith Cultural Education

Design community-level cultural education workshops drawing on the syncretic model demonstrated at Rewalsar — where Buddhist, Hindu, and Sikh traditions co-exist. Use the three-site narrative (Mandi → Dharamshala → Rewalsar) as an educational framework connecting distinct but interlinked heritage streams.

Conclusion

Preserving the Living Memory of the Himalayas

The cultural heritage of Himachal Pradesh — spanning the Shaiva temple traditions of Mandi (Site Visit 1), the Tibetan Buddhist practices of Dharamshala and McLeodganj (Site Visit 2), and the profound inter-faith sacred landscape of Rewalsar (Site Visit 3) — represents a convergence of civilizational knowledge streams of immeasurable depth and continuing vitality.

Our three-site field study traced a remarkable cultural arc: from the stone inscriptions of Trilokinath and the five-faced Shiva of Panchvaktra Temple in Mandi, to the morning prayers and Sowa-Rigpa healing traditions of McLeodganj's monasteries, to the sacred lotus lake of Rewalsar where the legend of Princess Mandarava — first encountered at the Kuan Rani Temple in Mandi — finds its miraculous culmination. These three sites form an interconnected sacred geography that has shaped human life, devotion, and knowledge in this Himalayan landscape for over five hundred years.

"Culture is not a relic of the past to be stored in a museum — it is a living conversation between generations. What we archive today, we gift to the future."

— Field Reflection, ISTP Cultural Heritage Project, IIT Mandi 2025-26 · Mandi · Dharamshala · Rewalsar

This heritage faces a critical inflection point. The generation that carries this knowledge in embodied, living form is aging, while the structural mechanisms for transmission are under sustained pressure from modernization and displacement. The window for systematic archiving across all three sites is narrowing, and urgent, collaborative, cross-community action is required. We hope this report serves as both a document of record and a call to sustained, collaborative action.
